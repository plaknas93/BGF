\chapter{Data Generating Process
(DGP)}\label{data-generating-process-dgp}

A data generating process (DGP) is a statistical model that describes
how data is generated. It is a theoretical construct that helps us
understand the underlying patterns and relationships in the data. In
practice, we often do not know the true DGP, and we must rely on
assumptions and approximations to make predictions.

Examples of DGPs include linear regression models, time series models,
and stochastic processes. Understanding the DGP is crucial for selecting
appropriate forecasting techniques and for interpreting the results of
forecasts.

\section{True DGP vs.~Models}\label{true-dgp-vs.-models}

True DGP refers to the actual process that generates the data, while
models are simplified representations of the DGP that we use for
forecasting. Models may not capture all the complexities of the true
DGP, but they can still provide useful insights and predictions. It is
important to evaluate the performance of models against the true DGP to
ensure their accuracy and reliability.

\subsection{What do we see?}\label{what-do-we-see}

The scatter plot suggests a \textbf{positive relationship} between study
hours and examination scores.

Students who study more tend to obtain higher scores.

However, the relationship is \textbf{not perfectly deterministic}.

For example, students who study a similar number of hours do not
necessarily obtain exactly the same score.

This suggests that something systematic is happening, but there is also
some randomness in the data.

\subsection{Signal and Noise}\label{signal-and-noise}

We can think of the observed score as consisting of two components:

\[
\boxed{\text{Observed Data} = \text{Signal} + \text{Noise}}
\]

\textbf{\emph{Signal}}

The \textbf{signal} is the systematic pattern in the data.

In our example, the signal is the tendency for examination scores to
increase as study hours increase.

\textbf{\emph{Noise}}

The \textbf{noise} is the random variation around the systematic
pattern.

Even if two students study for approximately the same amount of time,
their scores may differ because of:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Prior knowledge
\item
  Individual ability
\item
  Concentration
\item
  Exam difficulty
\item
  Luck
\item
  Other factors
\end{itemize}

Therefore, we do not expect every observation to lie exactly on the
underlying relationship.

\subsection{Capturing the Signal with Linear
Regression}\label{capturing-the-signal-with-linear-regression}

One simple way to capture the systematic component of the relationship
is \textbf{linear regression}.

We specify:

\[
Score_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 StudyHours_i + \varepsilon_i
\]

where:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\beta_0\) represents the intercept
\item
  \(\beta_1\) represents the effect of study hours
\item
  \(\varepsilon_i\) represents the noise
\end{itemize}

The regression model attempts to identify the \textbf{signal} while
treating the remaining variation as noise.

In estimated form:

\[
\widehat{Score}_i
=
\hat{\beta}_0
+
\hat{\beta}_1 StudyHours_i
\]

\subsection{Interpreting the Model}\label{interpreting-the-model}

The estimated regression equation provides a simple representation of
the systematic relationship between study hours and examination scores.

\textbf{\emph{Intercept}}

The intercept represents the model's predicted score when study hours
are zero.

\textbf{\emph{Slope}}

The slope represents the expected change in examination score associated
with one additional hour of study.

A positive slope indicates that \textbf{higher study hours are
associated with higher examination scores}.

\textbf{\emph{Residuals}}

The difference between the actual score and the score predicted by the
regression model is the \textbf{residual}:

\[
e_i = Score_i - \widehat{Score}_i
\]

These residuals represent the portion of the observed data that is
\textbf{not explained by the systematic relationship captured by the
model}.

\subsection{From Model to DGP}\label{from-model-to-dgp}

We can now return to our original question:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{What process generated the observed data?}
\end{quote}

A simple representation is:

\[
\boxed{
Score_i
=
\underbrace{\beta_0+\beta_1 StudyHours_i}_{\text{Signal}}
+
\underbrace{\varepsilon_i}_{\text{Noise}}
}
\]

The regression model gives us an \textbf{estimate of the systematic
component}, while the residuals provide an estimate of the unexplained
component.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The Data Generating Process (DGP) is the underlying process that
generates the observed data.}
\end{quote}

In this example, we conceptualize the DGP as a systematic relationship
between study hours and examination scores, together with a random
component.

\subsection{The Big Picture}\label{the-big-picture}

The important distinction is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{DGP}
\rightarrow
\text{Signal + Noise}
\rightarrow
\text{Observed Data}
}
\]

We observe the \textbf{data}.

We try to infer the underlying \textbf{signal}.

What remains unexplained is treated as \textbf{noise}.

This idea is fundamental to forecasting.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Forecasting is essentially an attempt to identify the systematic
component of a data-generating process and use it to predict future
observations.}
\end{quote}

In time-series forecasting, the signal may take different forms:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Level}
\item
  \textbf{Trend}
\item
  \textbf{Seasonality}
\item
  \textbf{Cycles}
\end{itemize}

The challenge is to distinguish these systematic patterns from random
noise.

\chapter{Naïve forecasting
Techniques}\label{nauxefve-forecasting-techniques}

Naïve forecasting techniques are simple methods that use historical data
to make predictions about future values. These techniques are often used
as a baseline for comparison with more complex forecasting methods. In
this section, we will explore some of the most commonly used naïve
forecasting techniques, including the naïve method, seasonal naïve
method, and drift method. We will also discuss their advantages and
limitations, as well as how to implement them in practice.

\section{Naïve Method}\label{nauxefve-method}

Naive method is a simple forecasting technique that assumes that the
future value of a time series will be equal to the most recent observed
value. This method is often used as a benchmark for evaluating the
performance of more complex forecasting methods. It is particularly
useful for time series data that exhibit a random walk behavior, where
the best prediction for the next value is simply the last observed
value.

\chapter{Smoothing Forecasting
Techniques}\label{smoothing-forecasting-techniques}

Smoothing forecasting techniques are a set of methods that use weighted
averages of past observations to make predictions about future values.
These techniques are particularly useful for time series data that
exhibit trends or seasonality. In this section, we will explore some of
the most commonly used smoothing forecasting techniques, including
moving average methods, exponential smoothing methods, Holt method, and
Holt-Winters methods. We will also discuss their advantages and
limitations, as well as how to implement them in practice.

\section{Moving Average Methods}\label{moving-average-methods}

Moving average methods are a simple smoothing technique that calculates
the average of a fixed number of past observations to make predictions
about future values. The moving average can be calculated using
different window sizes, which determines how many past observations are
included in the average. This method is particularly useful for time
series data that exhibit short-term fluctuations or noise, as it helps
to smooth out the data and reveal underlying trends.

\section{Weighted Moving Average
(WMA)}\label{weighted-moving-average-wma}

Weighted Moving Average (WMA) is a variation of the simple moving
average that assigns different weights to past observations, giving more
importance to recent data points. This method is particularly useful
when recent observations are believed to be more relevant for
forecasting future values. The weights can be assigned based on various
criteria, such as linear or exponential decay.

The core intuition behind the Weighted Moving Average (WMA) comes down
to a simple reality of most real-world data: what happened yesterday is
usually a better predictor of tomorrow than what happened last month.

While a Simple Moving Average (SMA) treats every period equally---giving
an observation from 10 quarters ago the exact same voting power as the
most recent quarter---a WMA shifts the balance of power.

\textbf{\emph{The Intuitive Analogy: Performance Reviews}}

Imagine evaluating a team member's performance:

\textbf{Simple Moving Average:} You take the average of their
performance over the last 10 years. An error made in Year 1 penalizes
them just as heavily as an error made last week.

\textbf{\emph{Weighted Moving Average:}} You evaluate the full 10-year
period, but you give 80\% of the weight to their recent 2 years of work
and 20\% to the older years. This reflects their current skill level
much more accurately.

\textbf{\emph{Why WMA Beats SMA for Recent Shifts}}

🎯 Reduces Lag: Because SMAs treat all points equally, they are slow to
react when a trend changes. WMas ``hug'' recent structural shifts much
faster.

⚖️ Controlled Memory: By choosing how fast the weights decay (e.g.,
linear vs.~exponential), you control how quickly the model ``forgets''
distant history.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulation of
WMA}\label{mathematical-formulation-of-wma}

For a 3-period moving average:

\[ F_t=\frac{Y_{t-1}+Y_{t-2}+Y_{t-3}}{3} \]

Each observation receives the same weight:

\[ \frac13,\frac13,\frac13 \]

In a Weighted Moving Average, we deliberately give different weights:

\[ F_t=w_1Y_{t-1}+w_2Y_{t-2}+w_3Y_{t-3} \]

where:

\[ w_1+w_2+w_3=1 \]

For example, we might give the most recent observation the greatest
weight:

\[ \boxed{F_t=0.5Y_{t-1}+0.3Y_{t-2}+0.2Y_{t-3}} \]

So the intuition is simply:

\textbf{Moving Average:} Every observation matters equally.

\textbf{Weighted Moving Average:} Recent observations can matter more.

\chapter{Trend \& Seasonality Forecasting
Techniques}\label{trend-seasonality-forecasting-techniques}

Trend and seasonality forecasting techniques are methods that account
for long-term trends and seasonal patterns in time series data. These
techniques are particularly useful for data that exhibit systematic
changes over time, such as sales data that may increase during certain
seasons or years. In this section, we will explore some of the most
commonly used trend and seasonality forecasting techniques, including
Holt's linear trend method and Holt-Winters seasonal method. We will
also discuss their advantages and limitations, as well as how to
implement them in practice.

\section{Holt's Exponential Smoothing Model (Double Exponential
Smoothing)}\label{holts-exponential-smoothing-model-double-exponential-smoothing}

Holt's model extends \textbf{Simple Exponential Smoothing} by explicitly
incorporating a \textbf{trend component}.

While Simple Exponential Smoothing is designed for data with a
relatively stable level, Holt's model is useful when the series shows a
systematic upward or downward movement over time.

The model therefore estimates two components:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  📍 \textbf{Level:} The current underlying value of the series\\
\item
  📈 \textbf{Trend:} The direction and rate of change over time
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The intuition is simple:}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Simple Exponential Smoothing:} What is the current level?
\textbf{Holt:} What is the current level + where is it heading?
\end{quote}

\subsection{Mathematical Formulation of Holt's
Model}\label{mathematical-formulation-of-holts-model}

The level and trend are updated recursively:

\[
L_t = \alpha Y_t + (1-\alpha)(L_{t-1}+T_{t-1})
\]

\[
T_t = \beta(L_t-L_{t-1})+(1-\beta)T_{t-1}
\]

The forecast is:

\[
\boxed{F_{t+h}=L_t+hT_t}
\]

where:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\alpha\) = smoothing parameter for the level
\item
  \(\beta\) = smoothing parameter for the trend
\item
  \(h\) = number of periods ahead
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}

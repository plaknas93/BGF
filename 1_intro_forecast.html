Humans have always wanted to know the future.

Long before statistical models, machine learning and forecasting software, people used a variety of methods to predict what was yet to happen.

2.3 From Prediction to Forecasting

Today, we still ask exactly the same question.

Can we use observable evidence and historical data to make predictions

This is where forecasting begins.

3 What is Forecasting?

Forecasting is the process of using information available today to estimate what is likely to happen in the future.

The information may include:

  • Historical data
  • Current conditions
  • Known future events
  • Relevant business information
WarningWarning

Forecast ≠ Certainty

A forecast is not a statement of what will happen.

It is an estimate of what is likely to happen, given the information available at the time the forecast is made.

3.1 Examples

Forecast Decision
Future product demand How much should we produce?
Electricity demand How much capacity is required?
Customer arrivals How many employees are needed?
Sales revenue How much inventory should we maintain?
Passenger demand How many flights should be scheduled?

3.2 Myths about Forecasting

Forecast is not a statement of what will happen. It is an estimate of what is likely to happen, given the information available at the time the forecast is made.

A Forecast is not a plan.

  • Forecast = what is likely to happen
  • Goal = what we want to happen
  • Planning = actions taken in response to forecasts and goals.

4 Why Do We Forecast?

The future is uncertain — but decisions cannot always wait for certainty.

Forecasting helps organisations make better decisions by providing an evidence-based estimate of what is likely to happen.

4.1 From uncertainty to action

Future is uncertain ↓ Forecast what is likely to happen ↓ Make a better-informed decision

6 What makes a good forecast?

Not everything is equally predictable

Tomorrow’s sunrise → highly predictable

Tomorrow’s lottery numbers → essentially unpredictable

So the important question is not:

“Can we forecast?”

but:

“How forecastable is the thing we are trying to predict?”

6.1 Four Conditions for Forecastability

  1. Understanding of relevant drivers: Do we understand the important factors associated with the outcome?

  2. Data availability: Do we have sufficient relevant historical information?

  3. Similarity between past and future: Is it reasonable to expect the future to behave sufficiently like the past?

  4. Forecast non-interference: Does the forecast itself materially change the behaviour that determines the outcome?

6.2 Example

6.2.1 Two Contrasting Forecasting Problems

Dimension ⚡ Electricity Demand 💱 Currency Exchange Rate
1. Understanding of relevant drivers Relatively well understood — temperature, calendar effects and economic conditions are important drivers. Limited — exchange rates are influenced by many economic, financial and political factors that are difficult to fully model.
2. Data availability High — several years of demand data and extensive weather data are typically available. High — extensive historical exchange-rate data are available.
3. Similarity between past & future Relatively high for short-term forecasts — demand behaviour tends to remain reasonably stable over a few weeks. Low — financial or political events can cause the future to differ substantially from the past.
4. Forecast non-interference High — published demand forecasts generally have little effect on residential electricity consumption. Low — forecasts can influence market participants and therefore affect exchange rates themselves.
Overall forecastability 🟢 Relatively high 🔴 Very low

7 What is a Time Series?

A time series is a sequence of observations recorded over time.

Examples:

  • Annual company profits
  • Quarterly sales
  • Monthly rainfall
  • Weekly retail sales
  • Daily stock prices
  • Hourly electricity demand

Ordinarily, we are only concerned with the relationship between the two variables. TS takes it one step further by examining the temporal dynamics of this relationship.

7.1 Time Series vs Cross-Sectional Data

Time Series Cross-Sectional
Observations Same variable over time Different entities at one point in time
Example RIL monthly sales, 2015–2025 Sales of 100 companies in 2025
Primary question What happens next? How do entities differ?

7.2 Example: Monthly Sales

Suppose a retailer records monthly sales for five years.

Month Sales (₹ lakh)
Jan 2022 82
Feb 2022 79
Mar 2022 91
… …
Dec 2026 156

The objective is to use the historical sequence to estimate:

What are sales likely to be in January 2027?

The correlation coefficient ranges from:

\[ -1 \leq r \leq 1 \]

where:

  • \(r \approx +1\) → strong positive linear association
  • \(r \approx 0\) → little linear association
  • \(r \approx -1\) → strong negative linear association

8.5 Model Diagnostics and Interpretation

8.5.1 Model Fit

F statistic:

The model fit is assessed using the F statistic. The F statistic tests the null hypothesis that all regression coefficients are equal to zero. A significant F statistic indicates that at least one explanatory variable is significantly associated with the dependent variable.

🔑Key Takeaway: The F statistic helps determine if the model as a whole is statistically significant.

8.5.2 Goodness of Fit

R-Squared:

The extent to which the model explains the variation in the dependent variable is measured by R-squared. R-squared ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a better fit. However, R-squared alone does not indicate whether the model is appropriate or whether the coefficients are significant.

Adjusted R-Squared:

A limitation with R-squared is that it always increases as more variables are added to the model, even if those variables do not contribute meaningfully.

Adjusted R-squared accounts for the number of explanatory variables in the model and is a more reliable measure of goodness of fit, especially when comparing models with different numbers of independent variables.

🔑Key Takeaway: R-squared and adjusted R-squared provide insight into how well the model explains the variation in the dependent variable, but they should be interpreted alongside other diagnostic measures.The higher the values, the better the model fits the data.

AIC, BIC, and HQIC:

Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and Hannan-Quinn Information Criterion (HQIC) are all used to compare different models. Lower values indicate a better model fit, accounting for the number of parameters in the model.

🔑Key Takeaway: AIC, BIC, and HQIC are useful for model selection, with lower values indicating a better balance between model fit and complexity.

8.5.3 Summary: Model fit and Goodness of Fit

Metric What does it tell us? How to interpret?
📊 F-statistic Tests whether the model is statistically significant as a whole. Significant F → at least one explanatory variable is associated with the dependent variable.
🎯 R² Measures the proportion of variation in the dependent variable explained by the model. Higher R² → greater explanatory power. However, it does not establish that the model is appropriate or coefficients are significant.
⚖️ Adjusted R² R² adjusted for the number of explanatory variables. Useful when comparing models with different numbers of predictors. Unlike R², it does not automatically increase when variables are added. Higher is generally better.
🔎 AIC Compares model fit while penalizing model complexity. Lower AIC → preferred model
🧮 BIC Similar to AIC but applies a stronger penalty for additional parameters. Lower BIC → preferred model
📐 HQIC Another information criterion balancing model fit and complexity. Lower HQIC → preferred model

8.6 Model Results Interpretation

8.6.1 Variable Significance

p-values: The p values determine the statistical significance of each independent variable. A low p-value (typically < 0.05) indicates that the variable is significantly associated with the dependent variable, while a high p-value suggests that the variable may not be a meaningful predictor.

Beta coefficients: The beta coefficients represent the estimated change in the dependent variable for a one-unit change in the independent variable, holding all other variables constant. The sign of the coefficient indicates the direction of the relationship.

8.6.2 Summary: Variable Significance

Metric What does it tell us? How to interpret?
🧪 p-value Tests whether an individual coefficient is statistically different from zero. p < 0.05 → statistically significant evidence of an association. p ≥ 0.05 → insufficient evidence of an association.
📈 β Coefficient Measures the estimated change in Y for a one-unit change in X, holding other variables constant. Sign → direction of relationship. Magnitude → size of the estimated effect.

10 📊 Time-Series Analysis

10.1 🔍 Looking Inside a Time Series: Decomposition

A time series is rarely just a collection of random observations.

When we observe a variable over time, its movements may reflect several underlying patterns.

For our quarterly cement sales, we might observe:

  • 📈 a long-term increase in sales,
  • 🔄 a recurring quarterly pattern,
  • 🌊 longer-term fluctuations, and
  • ⚡ unexpected shocks.

Time-series decomposition gives us a way to separate these different sources of variation.

💡 Think of decomposition as taking a complicated time series apart to understand what is happening inside it.

10.1.1 🧩 The Four Components of a Time Series

Component Icon The question we are asking
Trend 📈 Is the series moving upward or downward over the long run?
Seasonality 🔄 Does a pattern repeat at a regular frequency?
Cycle 🌊 Are there longer-term rises and falls?
Irregular ⚡ What unusual movements remain unexplained?

10.2 🧠 The Big Picture

Think of an observed time series as the result of several underlying patterns working together:

\[ \boxed{\text{Observed} = \text{Trend} + \text{Seasonality} + \text{Irregular}} \]

The additive model assumes that the components contribute independently in absolute terms. For example, seasonal fluctuations remain roughly constant in size over time.

Note:
1. A time series may also contain a cycle component. However, for simplicity, we will focus on the three core components: trend, seasonality, and irregular variation (also called randomness, noise, or random noise).
2. A time series may be additive or multiplicative, depending on how the components interact with one another. The above equation represents an additive model. The multiplicative model is beyond the scope of this course.

10.3 TS Decomposition in Python

We will now use Python to decompose our quarterly cement sales time series into its underlying components: trend, seasonality, and irregular variation.

Tip💻Coding Session 
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import pandas as pd

# Convert Quarter to datetime
df["Quarter"] = pd.PeriodIndex(df["Quarter"], freq="Q").to_timestamp()

# Create time series
ts = df.set_index("Quarter")["Cement_Sales_000_Tons"]

# Decompose
decomposition = seasonal_decompose(ts, model="additive", period=4)

# Plot
fig, axes = plt.subplots(4, 1, figsize=(12, 10))

axes[0].plot(ts)
axes[0].set_title("Observed")

axes[1].plot(decomposition.trend)
axes[1].set_title("Trend")

axes[2].plot(decomposition.seasonal)
axes[2].set_title("Seasonal")

axes[3].plot(decomposition.resid)
axes[3].set_title("Irregular")

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
